## 2. There is a published meta-analysis directly bearing on Falsification Condition 3

**This is the most significant finding, and it is a substantive point about the theory.**

MST states that its claims must be narrowed or abandoned if *"relational systems show no
emergent cognition beyond their components."*

There is a preregistered systematic review and meta-analysis testing something very close to
this, and it is by **Thomas Malone** — one of the four neighbours the manuscript names:

> Vaccaro, M., Almaatouq, A., & Malone, T. (2024). "When combinations of humans and AI are
> useful: A systematic review and meta-analysis." *Nature Human Behaviour*, 8(12), 2293–2303.
> 106 experimental studies, 370 effect sizes.

**Headline finding: on average, human–AI combinations performed significantly *worse* than the
best of humans or AI alone.**

Disaggregated:
- **Losses** on tasks involving *decision-making*.
- **Gains** on tasks involving *content creation*.
- Gains when the human outperformed the AI alone; losses when the AI outperformed the human.

### Why this matters to the manuscript

The manuscript does not cite it. It is the closest thing in the published empirical literature
to a test of MST's own third falsification condition, and on its face it reads as
counter-evidence. A reviewer who knows the field will raise it. Better to meet it first.

### Why it is not actually a clean refutation — and why the distinction is worth making explicit

MST's condition is carefully worded and the meta-analysis does not quite measure it. MST
requires *epistemic novelty at the relational level* — "new questions, uncertainties or
capabilities arising from the relation itself" — and explicitly says that "mere connection,
memory or tool use is not sufficient." Vaccaro et al. measure **task performance synergy** on
largely bounded benchmark tasks. Performance on a decision task is not epistemic novelty.
These are different quantities, and MST is entitled to say so.

But it is only entitled to say so *if it says so.* Right now the falsification condition is
open to being read as a performance claim, which the literature would then appear to defeat.
A clause distinguishing relational epistemic novelty from task-performance synergy would close
this, and cost nothing.

### The stronger move: the disaggregation may support MST

The split finding is the interesting part. Combinations **lose** on decision tasks and **gain**
on generative ones. That is at least suggestive of exactly the shape MST predicts — the
relation contributing where novelty is generated, and subtracting where a decision is being
made under automation bias. The manuscript's Chapter V argument about human judgment degrading
under automated recommendation and Law VII's "practical capacity to refuse" both point at the
same phenomenon from the other direction.

If that reading holds, the strongest empirical evidence against MST's emergence claim is
partly evidence for its judgment claim. That is worth a paragraph in the manuscript, and it is
the basis of the rewritten Malone letter.
