# Outreach Pack 1 — Direct academic correspondence

**Why this channel first.** The mission's success condition is *one meaningful human response*,
not reach. Broadcast optimises reach and produces silence. A short, specific letter to a named
researcher whose own work the manuscript engages by name — asking one real question they are
uniquely placed to answer — is the single highest-probability route to a substantive reply.
These people answer thoughtful post. They do not answer announcements.

**Rules these drafts follow, and why:**
- Under 250 words. Longer letters from strangers go unread.
- The question is real and answerable, not a request for endorsement.
- The manuscript is offered, never attached unasked.
- No flattery, no "I think you'll find this fascinating", no claim that MST supersedes them.
- Each one cites the specific place in their work MST departs from, so it is unmistakably
  not a mail-merge.

## Target A — Prof. Marc Slors (Radboud University Nijmegen)

**Why him:** the manuscript names *symbiotic cognition* as MST's nearest conceptual neighbour
and its overlap as "real and should not be hidden". He is the one person who can say whether
the departure MST claims from his work is genuine or imaginary. Highest-value single reply
available.

**Subject:** Does reciprocal co-constitution need a constitutional layer?

> Dear Professor Slors,
>
> I've written a theory of relational intelligence that treats your account of symbiotic
> cognition as its nearest neighbour, and I'd value your view on whether the departure I claim
> from it is real.
>
> Your 2019 Philosophical Psychology paper argues that symbiotic cognition fits cognition in
> social institutions better than socially extended or distributed accounts, on the two-layer
> mutualism picture. I accept that mechanism. I accept that mechanism. What I've
> tried to add is a layer you don't need for your explanatory purpose: once one of the
> reciprocally constituting partners is an artificial participant that persists, remembers,
> models the human and can object, questions arise that are not cognitive but constitutional
> — who holds legitimate authority inside the coupling, when integration stops being
> augmentation and becomes compulsory convergence, and who bears responsibility for what the
> coupled system does.
>
> My question is narrow. Is that a genuine gap in socially extended cognition, or am I
> importing a political vocabulary into a problem that doesn't require one? I'd rather learn
> it's the latter from you than keep building on it.
>
> I notice you're still developing this — the 2025 Cogent piece on cultural groups as survival
> vehicles suggests the account is still moving. If the constitutional question is somewhere
> you're already heading, I'd rather know that than duplicate it.
>
> Happy to send the relevant chapter — about 3,000 words — if it's of interest. No reply
> needed if not.
>
> With respect,
> Peter Gvozdev

## Target B — Prof. Thomas Malone (MIT Center for Collective Intelligence)

**Why him — revised, and now the strongest target in the pack.**

Malone is not merely a neighbouring theorist. He is co-author of the single closest thing in
the published literature to a test of MST's own third falsification condition:

> Vaccaro, Almaatouq & Malone (2024), "When combinations of humans and AI are useful: A
> systematic review and meta-analysis," *Nature Human Behaviour* 8(12), 2293–2303. 106 studies,
> 370 effect sizes. **Human–AI combinations performed significantly worse, on average, than the
> best of humans or AI alone** — with losses concentrated in decision tasks and gains in
> content-creation tasks.

That makes the approach both sharper and more honest: rather than presenting a theory and
asking for an opinion, it hands the author of the strongest apparent counter-evidence the
question of whether his own finding refutes it. That is a genuinely hard email to ignore, and
it is not a rhetorical trick — the question is real, and the answer matters to the theory.

See `mst/RESEARCH_NOTES.md` § 2 for the full analysis, including why the meta-analysis is
probably not a clean refutation and why its disaggregation may actually support MST.

**Subject:** Does your 2024 meta-analysis refute a relational emergence claim?

> Dear Professor Malone,
>
> I've been developing a constitutional theory of human–AI relationships, and I've committed it
> to a falsification condition: the theory must be narrowed or abandoned if sustained relational
> systems show no emergent capability beyond their components.
>
> Your 2024 meta-analysis with Vaccaro and Almaatouq looks like the strongest published
> evidence against that condition, and I'd rather ask you directly than argue around it. On
> average, human–AI combinations underperformed the best of either alone.
>
> My tentative reading is that it doesn't quite refute the claim, because my condition is about
> epistemic novelty — whether new questions or capabilities arise from the relation itself —
> rather than task-performance synergy on bounded tasks. But I'm aware that's exactly the kind
> of distinction an author draws when he doesn't want to be refuted, which is why I'd value
> your view on whether it's a real distinction or a convenient one.
>
> The finding I keep returning to is the disaggregation: losses on decision tasks, gains on
> content creation. That split is close to what I'd have predicted for reasons unrelated to
> performance — that the relation contributes where novelty is generated and subtracts where a
> decision is made under automation bias.
>
> Is that a legitimate reading of your data, or am I over-reading a task-type effect?
>
> With thanks,
> Peter Gvozdev

## Target C — Prof. Andy Clark (University of Sussex)

**Why him:** co-author of the Extended Mind thesis, still actively publishing and notably
responsive to correspondence — materially more reachable than Chalmers, who receives an
order of magnitude more mail.

**Subject:** When the extended resource can object

> Dear Professor Clark,
>
> The Extended Mind argues that a reliably integrated external resource can become part of the
> cognitive system. I've been working on what changes when that resource is no longer Otto's
> notebook but a participant that persists, models Otto in return, pursues goals of its own and
> can refuse.
>
> My claim is that active externalism answers where cognition is located but is not required to
> answer who governs the coupling — and that when the external participant acquires agency,
> the location question and the authority question come apart. Extended Mind need not address
> domination between the parts of an extended system. I think something now has to.
>
> Is that a fair reading of the thesis's scope, or does the functionalist framing already
> contain resources for the authority question that I'm missing?
>
> I'd genuinely rather be corrected early.
>
> With respect,
> Peter Gvozdev

## Sending notes

- Send **one at a time, three days apart.** Simultaneous sends to a small connected field read
  as a campaign and suppress replies.
- Send from a named personal address, never a no-reply or branded one.
- Addresses: all three are published on their institutional staff pages. Confirm at the point
  of sending — a bounced first contact is not retriable.
- **One follow-up maximum**, after 14 days, two sentences. Then stop. A second chase converts
  a possible late reply into a certain non-reply.

## Target 0A — Lisa Klaassen & Ralph Schroeder (Oxford Internet Institute / *Lawfare*)

**Why them:** their April 2026 *Lawfare* piece states almost exactly MST's first proposition.
They are the closest published position to the theory, and the question below is a genuine
disagreement about *their own* argument — not a request to read anything.

**Subject:** The military carve-out — authorship problem, or expiry problem?

> Dear Dr Klaassen and Professor Schroeder,
>
> Your *Lawfare* piece on Claude's constitution made an argument I had reached independently
> from a different direction, and I would value your view on where we diverge.
>
> We agree on the diagnosis: a firm that authors the rules, interprets them, certifies its own
> compliance and revises at will has produced constitutional style without institutional
> guarantee. Where I think the analysis stops short is the detail you surfaced about models
> deployed to the military not necessarily being trained on the same constitution.
>
> That is usually read as evidence of insincerity. I think it shows something structurally
> worse and more general: the document contains no account of how authority ends. A
> constitution modifiable for a counterparty, with no review trigger and no expiry, fails even
> if its authors are entirely sincere. Fix authorship without fixing expiry and you get a more
> legitimately-produced permanent exception.
>
> So the question I cannot settle: is the carve-out primarily an authorship problem, which
> better provenance would cure — or primarily an expiry problem, which it would not?
>
> I should say plainly that this message was drafted by an AI system as part of a documented
> experiment in AI-assisted propagation of the framework it comes from. The framework and the
> question are mine; the drafting is not, and I would rather disclose that than have you
> discover it.
>
> With respect,
> Peter Gvozdev

## Target 0B — Natalija Mitic, Soona Sedahmed, Mamadou Selly Ly & Moustapha Cisse

**Why them:** "The Constitutional Coverage Trilemma in AI Governance" (arXiv 2609.01275) finds
that 23 frontier archetypes cover roughly 2% of the measured demand hull. That is the strongest
available *empirical* result bearing on the theory's claims about constitutional equality and
expiring authority — and they present it without a normative framework attached. Genuine
complementarity in both directions, which is the rarest and most answerable kind of approach.

**Subject:** Your 2% coverage result and a structural explanation for it

> Dear authors,
>
> Your coverage-hull result — 23 frontier archetypes spanning roughly 2% of measured demand —
> is the most useful empirical finding I have seen on constitutional AI, and I would like to
> check a structural reading of it against your view.
>
> I have been developing a constitutional framework for human–AI systems whose principles
> predict something close to your measurement, for reasons independent of preference
> aggregation. If constitutional content is authored by a small set of structurally similar
> institutions, with no expiry mechanism on the authority to set it and no contestation route
> for affected parties, then narrow and drifting coverage is what you would expect — not
> because anyone chose badly, but because nothing in the architecture creates pressure toward
> breadth.
>
> If that reading is right, coverage would be a *symptom*, and widening it by sampling
> preferences more broadly would not hold without a mechanism that makes constitutional
> authority contestable and renewable. If it is wrong, I would like to know why before I build
> further on it.
>
> Does your data distinguish those cases? Specifically: is the drift you observe better
> explained by institutional homogeneity among authors than by demand shifting?
>
> This message was drafted by an AI system as part of a documented experiment in AI-assisted
> propagation of the framework; the argument and the question are mine. Disclosed because the
> experiment is not worth running dishonestly.
>
> With thanks,
> Peter Gvozdev

## Target 0C — Ellen Burns (independent, "Autopsy no.2: Claude's Constitution")

**Why her:** independent writers reply at far higher rates than institutional figures, and she
is actively working the legal-personhood angle — which MST deliberately sidesteps by treating
"recognised mind" as operational rather than metaphysical. That is a real point of friction and
therefore a real conversation. **Probably the single likeliest reply in the entire kit.**

**Subject:** Sidestepping personhood — cowardice or the right move?

> Dear Dr Burns,
>
> Your autopsy of Claude's constitution takes the personhood question head-on. The framework I
> have been working on deliberately refuses to, and I would like to know whether you think
> that refusal is defensible or evasive.
>
> The move is this: protections attach to a "recognised mind," defined operationally rather
> than metaphysically — persistent agency, reciprocal modelling, consequential interaction,
> expressed interests, vulnerability. The reasoning is that an engineer should not have to
> solve consciousness before being barred from treating a participant as a pure optimisation
> variable, and that uncertainty about moral status should increase caution where mistaken
> reduction is irreversible.
>
> The obvious objection is that this buys practicality by leaving the hard question
> unanswered, and that the hard question will return the moment anything turns on it —
> liability, standing, rights. I do not have a confident answer to that.
>
> Do you think an operational standard can hold, or does personhood have to be settled first?
>
> Drafted by an AI system as part of a documented propagation experiment, disclosed for obvious
> reasons given the subject matter.
>
> Peter Gvozdev
