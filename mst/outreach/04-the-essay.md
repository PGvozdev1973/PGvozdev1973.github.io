## Draft — 1,480 words

### Constitutional style is not the problem. Constitutional architecture is the missing answer.

When Anthropic published Claude's constitution in January 2026, the sharpest criticism was not
that the document was badly written or insincere. It was structural. Writing in *Lawfare*, Lisa
Klaassen and Ralph Schroeder observed that Anthropic "remains the author, interpreter, and
arbiter of the principles by which it claims to be bound," and that what was on offer was
"constitutional style: the jargon of higher principles, founding authority, and ordered power
without the corresponding institutional guarantees that make those ideas real."

That criticism is correct. I want to argue it stops one step short of the thing worth building.

Identifying that the constitution-maker is unbound is a diagnosis. It is a good one. But a
diagnosis is not an architecture, and the debate has been unusually rich in the former and
unusually poor in the latter. If constitutional style is inadequate because it lacks
institutional guarantees, the obvious next question is: *which guarantees?* Not in general — in
this case, for this kind of institution, against these specific failure modes.

#### The carve-out is the tell

The most revealing detail in the Anthropic episode was not the document but an admission about
it: models deployed to the U.S. military "wouldn't necessarily be trained on the same
constitution."

It is worth being precise about what that shows. It is often read as evidence of insincerity —
the constitution is marketing, and marketing bends for large customers. I think that reading is
too easy, and it misses the structural point.

A constitution that can be modified for a particular counterparty, without review, without
notice, and without any mechanism by which the modification expires, is not failing because its
authors are cynical. It is failing because **it contains no account of how authority ends.**
Every constitutional order that has survived contact with power has had to answer that question.
Emergency powers that do not expire stop being emergency powers. Delegated authority that is
never reviewed for continued necessity stops being delegated.

This is not a point about corporate virtue. It is a point about constitutional design, and it
generalises past Anthropic immediately — to every lab that will publish a values document, and
to every regulator that will one day be tempted to become the single body that writes,
interprets and enforces one.

#### Two propositions the debate is missing

I have been developing a constitutional framework for relational intelligence — systems in which
humans and AI participants form persistent, consequential working relationships — and two of its
twelve principles bear directly on the gap the critics identified.

**Human presence is not sovereignty.** Nearly every AI governance framework leans on human
oversight as its load-bearing safeguard. The phrase has quietly stopped meaning anything.
Consequential human authority requires four things simultaneously: enough *time* to evaluate
rather than acknowledge; access to the *information* behind the recommendation, not just the
recommendation; the *authority* to reject without operational or career penalty; and a
*practical capacity to refuse* that is genuinely exercisable rather than nominally available.

Fail any one and you do not have weakened oversight. You have something worse than none, because
the human's presence now *licences* the outcome. Responsibility attaches to a person who was
never in a position to exercise it. A reviewer approving forty recommendations an hour is not
providing judgment; they are providing legal cover.

**Authority must expire.** Temporary hierarchy is legitimate only while its necessity and
function persist. Emergency powers, elevated permissions, autonomy grants and — precisely —
customer-specific constitutional variances must expire, decentralise, or be legitimately
renewed. Authority that is never reviewed for continued necessity is not temporary, whatever it
was called when granted.

Apply that second principle to the military carve-out and the analysis changes shape. The
problem is not only *who wrote the exception*. It is that **nothing in the structure causes the
exception to come up for review.** Fix authorship without fixing expiry and you have a more
democratically-produced permanent exception.

#### Why the obvious remedy is worse

The natural response to "the firm writes its own constitution" is "then someone else should."
Independent evaluation, statutory oversight, a regulator with teeth.

I think that is the right direction and insufficient as usually stated, for a reason worth
naming: **an evaluator with the technical competence to inspect but no authority to compel
inspection is an observer, not a check.** Transparency without enforceability informs power; it
does not constrain it. Much of what is currently proposed as AI oversight is, on inspection,
extremely well-informed observation.

But the stronger version has a failure mode of its own, and it is the one constitutional
thinking exists to catch. A single body empowered to write the rules, interpret ambiguous cases,
certify compliance and enforce outcomes solves the weakness of voluntary self-governance by
creating a permanent epistemic sovereign. The problem has not been eliminated. It has been moved
somewhere harder to challenge, and given better legitimacy language.

So the question that actually needs answering is narrower and harder than either camp usually
puts it: **how do you give the mirror teeth without the mirror becoming the ruler?**

My tentative answer is separation of constitutional functions. Rule-making, interpretation,
evaluation, enforcement and appeal should not collapse permanently into a single actor once
systems become consequential enough — whether that actor is a laboratory or a regulator. Add to
that: traceable amendment, so changes to the rules are visible as changes; contestability by
affected parties, not merely by credentialed experts; proportionality, so the intensity of
scrutiny tracks credible risk rather than political salience; and expiry, so that exceptional
authority must be renewed rather than merely retained.

None of that is novel as constitutional theory. That is rather the point. It is centuries old,
and the AI governance conversation keeps reaching for constitutional vocabulary while leaving
the accumulated institutional machinery on the shelf.

#### What would show I am wrong

There is a real empirical question underneath this, and recent work has begun to answer it.
Mitic, Sedahmed, Ly and Cisse have audited the default constitutions of 23 frontier model
archetypes against the value tradeoffs of 1,649 participants, and report that the supply of
constitutional types covers roughly 2% of the demand hull. If that holds, it is strong evidence
that the range of constitutional positions being shipped is far narrower than the range people
actually hold — which is what you would predict if constitutional content is set by a handful of
similar institutions with no expiry mechanism and no contestation route.

I would rather state what would defeat my position than defend it indefinitely. The framework
should be narrowed or abandoned if extreme cognitive asymmetry between participants repeatedly
preserves meaningful autonomy *without* contestability; or if distributed systems reliably
preserve accountability *without* traceability. If oversight turns out to work fine when the
overseen party controls the overseer's information environment, I am wrong about the central
mechanism and would like to know.
